Farewell King Oyo: Tooro prepares for final send-off amid succession dispute

The kingdom prepares to bury Omukama Oyo Nyimba Kabamba Iguru Rukidi IV on Saturday, September 12, as mourners remember a monarch who spent more than three decades on the throne and a succession dispute continues to command national attention.

September 11, 2026 • Mbale Media Daily National Desk

Tooro Kingdom is preparing for one of the most significant moments in its recent history as the remains of King Oyo Nyimba Kabamba Iguru Rukidi IV are taken to the Karambi Royal Tombs for burial on Saturday, September 12.

The farewell comes just over two weeks after the king died in the United States at the age of 34. Oyo had been on the Tooro throne since September 12, 1995, when he was only three years old. His accession made him one of the most recognisable young monarchs in the world and he went on to lead the kingdom for more than 30 years.

A final journey marked by tradition and national attention

The funeral programme has been organised as a major cultural and state occasion. The king's remains returned to Uganda on September 4 and were taken to Kyegegwa before being transported to Fort Portal, where the body lay in state at Karuziika Palace during the mourning period.

On Saturday, the programme is expected to include prayers at St John's Cathedral in Fort Portal before the final procession to the Karambi Royal Tombs. The burial date is especially significant because September 12 is also the anniversary of Oyo's 1995 coronation. What was once a day associated with the celebration of his accession will instead become a day of mourning and remembrance.

A king remembered beyond the throne

Oyo's reign was closely associated with the preservation of Tooro's cultural identity and with efforts in areas including education, youth empowerment, environmental conservation, health and tourism. Government institutions and cultural leaders have described his death as a loss not only to the royal family and Batooro but to Uganda as a whole.

President Yoweri Museveni received the king's remains at Entebbe International Airport and paid tribute to Oyo's leadership. The government also took responsibility for preparations for an official burial, underlining the national significance attached to the final send-off.

The succession dispute

While preparations for the burial continue, the question of who should succeed Oyo has created a serious dispute within the royal family and the Babiito royal clan.

A royal succession process selected Prince Edward Rukidi Kijanangoma, a cousin of the late king and a former Uganda Broadcasting Corporation presenter and editor, as the preferred successor. The decision followed an earlier succession process involving Prince George Desmond Kamurasi, who was reported to have declined the throne.

Members of Oyo's immediate family, including Queen Mother Best Kemigisa and Princess Ruth Komuntale, have challenged the succession decision. They have pointed to a will attributed to the late king which, according to their account, provides for a biological son to succeed him if the child is lawfully recognised. The identity and eligibility of the alleged heir have become central to the dispute.

The competing claims have prompted calls for calm and a lawful, orderly resolution. The dispute has also raised questions about the relationship between traditional succession customs, the late king's reported wishes and the procedures used by the royal clan to identify a successor.

Why the succession matters

Tooro is a traditional kingdom and does not exercise sovereign political power, but the institution remains deeply important to the cultural identity of the Batooro. The monarch is a symbol of unity, heritage and continuity, while the kingdom also has a significant historical and cultural role in western Uganda.

The succession therefore carries importance beyond the question of who wears the crown. It is also about the continuity of the institution, the interpretation of tradition, the management of royal responsibilities and the confidence of the people of Tooro in their cultural leadership.

A kingdom says goodbye

As mourners gather in Fort Portal and across Tooro, Saturday's burial will close the formal mourning period for a king whose reign began in childhood and continued into adulthood. For many Batooro, the occasion will be both a farewell to a familiar monarch and a moment to reflect on the future of their kingdom.

The immediate succession dispute means that the farewell to Oyo is taking place alongside a new chapter whose outcome is still being contested. What happens next will be closely watched by the royal family, Batooro subjects and the wider Ugandan public.
